# How to Read a Lottery Result Chart: Series, Draw Number and Prize Tiers Explained

Every day, lakhs of people in India check lottery results — but many stare at the result chart and wonder what half of it means. What is a "series"? Why does the draw have a number? What does "5th prize — ₹120" really cover? If you have ever checked a result and felt unsure whether your ticket won anything, this guide is for you.

## What a Result Chart Actually Is

A result chart is the official published list of winning ticket numbers for one draw. It is published after the draw takes place and is later confirmed in the official gazette. The chart usually shows:

- The name of the lottery scheme (for example, Dear Morning, Dear Evening, or a Kerala weekly scheme)
- The draw date and time
- The draw number
- The ticket price
- The prize tiers, from first prize down to the consolation and lower prizes
- The winning numbers for each tier

Every number on the chart matters, but not every number is relevant to you. The trick is knowing where to look.

## The Draw Number: Which Draw Is This?

Every draw carries a unique draw number. Think of it as the draw's ID. For example, if you hold a ticket for a particular date, the result chart for that date will carry a specific draw number. Always match the draw number and the date on the chart with the draw number and date printed on your ticket. This is the single most common checking mistake: people check the right scheme but the wrong day's draw, and either celebrate early or miss a win.

A simple rule: date first, then draw number, then your ticket number. If the date or draw number does not match your ticket, stop — you are looking at the wrong chart.

## The Series: That Letter (or Letters) Before the Number

Most Indian lottery tickets carry a series — usually one or two letters, such as A, B, C, D or E — followed by a number. The series is part of your ticket's identity. A winning number in the first prize tier is only yours if both the series and the number match exactly.

Here is why this matters. In many draws, the first prize is awarded to one ticket in one series only. So a chart may list a first-prize number like "A 12345" — and a ticket numbered 12345 in series B has not won the first prize. For lower prize tiers, the rules vary by scheme: some tiers are series-independent, meaning any ticket ending in the listed digits wins regardless of series. Always read the tier's own note on the chart before concluding anything.

| Chart element | What it means | What to check on your ticket |
|---|---|---|
| Draw number | The unique ID of this draw | Same draw number printed on your ticket |
| Draw date and time | When the draw happened | Same date as your ticket |
| Series | The letter group your ticket belongs to | The series letter on your ticket |
| Ticket number | The number printed on your ticket | Exact match with the winning number |
| Prize tier | Which prize level a number belongs to | Which tier your match falls in |

## Prize Tiers: From First Prize Down

A result chart is organised in tiers, biggest prize first. Here is how the tiers typically work, using real Indian state lotteries as examples:

**First prize.** The headline prize. In Nagaland's Dear lotteries, the first prize is ₹1 Crore. In Kerala's daily draws, the first prize goes up to ₹1 Crore depending on the scheme. Only one ticket (or one ticket per series, depending on the scheme) wins this.

**Second and third prizes.** Smaller but still substantial — typically a few thousand to a few lakh rupees, depending on the scheme. These are awarded to a small number of tickets.

**Fourth and fifth prizes.** These go to many more tickets. In Nagaland's Dear draws, for example, the fifth prize is a small fixed amount awarded to tickets matching the last four digits of a set of listed numbers.

**Consolation prize.** Some schemes pay a consolation prize to tickets that match the first-prize number but carry a different series. This is exactly why the series matters — a near-miss on the series can still be worth money.

**Lower tiers (last-digit prizes).** Many charts end with a long list of numbers where only the last few digits matter. For example, a tier may pay a fixed small amount to every ticket whose last four digits match any of the listed four-digit numbers. This is where most small wins come from, and it is also where most people stop reading too early and miss their win.

## A Worked Example: Checking a Nagaland Dear Ticket

Let us walk through a real-style check. Suppose you bought a Nagaland Dear ticket:

- Scheme: Dear Evening, drawn at 6 PM
- Ticket price: ₹7
- Your ticket: series C, number 48213

Now you open the result chart for that day's 6 PM draw. Here is how to check, step by step:

1. **Confirm the date and draw number.** The chart must be for the same date and draw number as your ticket.
2. **Check the first prize.** Does the first-prize number match both your series (C) and your number (48213)? If yes, you have won the ₹1 Crore first prize.
3. **Check the consolation prize.** If your number matches the first-prize number but your series is different, check whether the scheme pays a consolation prize.
4. **Work down the tiers.** Check the second, third and fourth prizes the same way — full number and series where required.
5. **Check the last-digit tiers.** Finally, look at the last four digits of your ticket (8213). If 8213 appears in the fifth-prize list, you have won that tier's fixed amount.
6. **Stop only at the bottom.** A ticket can win only one tier — normally the highest tier it qualifies for — but you must check every tier before concluding you have won nothing.

This same method works for any state. A Kerala daily draw at 3 PM, a Sikkim Labhlaxmi draw at 4 PM, or a Punjab Dear 50 draw at 6:30 PM — the chart layout is the same, only the scheme names, ticket prices and prize amounts change.

## Same Draw, Different States: Why Charts Can Look Identical

One thing that confuses newcomers: the Nagaland Dear 6 PM draw and the Sikkim 6 PM draw often show the same winning numbers. That is because the 6 PM Dear draw is shared — Sikkim's 6 PM result mirrors Nagaland's. They are not two different draws that happen to agree; they are the same draw listed for two states. By contrast, Sikkim's own Labhlaxmi draw at 4 PM is a different, separate draw with its own plain four-digit numbers and its own ₹10,000 first prize.

Timings and prices vary by scheme, so always confirm the scheme printed on your ticket rather than relying on memory.

## Common Mistakes When Reading a Chart

- **Checking the wrong day's chart.** Always match the date first.
- **Ignoring the series.** A matching number in the wrong series is not a first-prize win.
- **Stopping at the first prize.** Most wins are in the lower tiers. Read to the bottom.
- **Trusting an unofficial screenshot.** Forwarded images can be edited or outdated. Verify against the official gazette or a trusted results page before acting.
- **Missing the claim window.** Prizes must generally be claimed within 30 days of the draw. Do not sit on a winning ticket.
- **Forgetting tax.** Prizes above ₹10,000 attract a flat 30% TDS. The amount you receive is after this deduction, plus applicable surcharge and cess.
